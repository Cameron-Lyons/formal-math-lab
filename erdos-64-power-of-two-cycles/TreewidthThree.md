# Cycles of length 4, 8 or 16 in partial 3-trees

**Theorem.** Let $H$ be a finite simple graph on at least three vertices. Let $x,y$ be distinct vertices. Suppose every vertex except possibly $x,y$ has degree at least three, and making $x,y$ adjacent gives a graph of treewidth at most three. Then $H$ contains a cycle of length 4, 8 or 16.

Consequently, in a nonempty graph $G$ of minimum degree three and treewidth at most three, such a cycle can avoid any prescribed edge $xy$. Apply the theorem to $H=G-xy$. The bound 16 is sharp.

Call a graph **clean** if it has no cycle of length 4, 8 or 16. The proof describes all the ways a clean graph can grow behind a triangle of a 3-tree. There are six shapes.

## Pieces with three boundary vertices

Complete $H+xy$ to a 3-tree on the same vertices. A 3-tree starts with a triangle and repeatedly adds a vertex adjacent to an existing triangle. We can start at a triangle containing $xy$. Indeed, the four-vertex cliques form a tree of bags when each new clique is attached to an earlier bag containing its parent triangle. The bags containing a given vertex stay connected. Root this tree at a bag containing $xy$ and read it from the root outwards. Each new bag adds its fourth vertex to the triangle shared with its parent. For three vertices the completion is already a triangle. [Bodlaender, Theorem 35](https://cgi.di.uoa.gr/~sgk/teaching/GT/Bodlaender98-bt.pdf)

A **piece** has three boundary vertices $a,b,c$, with all edges among them omitted. Its interior vertices have degree at least three. The pieces used below are precisely those generated by these three operations, read from the leaves to the root.

1. Start with three isolated vertices (the empty piece).
2. Combine two pieces by identifying their three boundary vertices in order. This combines branches growing from the same triangle.
3. Introduce $d$ on $abc$. Combine the pieces on $abd,acd,bcd$, add any subset of $da,db,dc$, and require $d$ to have degree at least three. The new boundary is $a,b,c$.

The interiors of the input pieces are disjoint, since every new triangle has a unique creator. An edge to an earlier vertex is supplied when its later endpoint is introduced. Only the three root edges remain to be added at the end.

## Six shapes and their paths

Four shapes come from graphs with three distinguished vertices called *ports*. All ports have degree two. To extend a port, attach one new leaf and use it as the boundary vertex in place of the old port.

* $T$ is a triangle with its three vertices as ports.
* To form $D(P)$, take two copies of a three-port graph $P$ with ports $u,v,w$. Add the edge $u_1u_2$ and the two-edge path $v_1mv_2$. The new ports are $w_1,w_2$ (the *outer* pair) and $m$ (the *middle* port).
* $H_7=D(T)$. To form $H_{15}=D(H_7)$, take $u,v$ to be the two outer ports of each $H_7$.
* $H'_7$ is $K_4$ with the three edges incident to one vertex subdivided once. The subdivision vertices are its ports.

**Claim.** Every clean piece is empty, a three-leaf star with its leaves on the boundary, or one of $T,H_7,H'_7,H_{15}$ with some ports extended. The triangle must have at least two ports extended so that the boundary is independent.

Here are all simple path lengths between ports before extension. The intervals contain every integer from the first endpoint to the last.

| Core | Outer pair | Each other pair | One length available for every pair while avoiding the third port |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $T$ | 1–2 | 1–2 | 1 |
| $H_7$ | 3–6 | 2–6 | 4 |
| $H'_7$ | 2–6 | 2–6 | 4 |
| $H_{15}$ | 5–14 | 3–14 | 8 |

For $D(P)$ let $I$ be the lengths from $u$ to $v$, and $J$ those from $w$ to either $u$ or $v$. Write $I+J$ for the set of sums of a length from each set. An outer-to-outer path uses either of the two links between copies, giving $J+J+\{1,2\}$. A middle-to-outer path starts on the target side or the opposite side, giving $(1+J)\cup(2+I+J)$. To avoid the third port, an outer-to-outer path can use the direct link. For a middle-to-outer path starting on the opposite side, choose its $v$-to-$u$ segment there to avoid $w$. This gives length $2+1+1=4$ in $H_7$ and $2+4+2=8$ in $H_{15}$. In $H'_7$ one can go through the center (length 2), along the outer triangle (3 or 4), or through the center and out the third spoke before following that triangle (5 or 6).

All four cores are clean. A cycle crossing between the copies of $P$ uses both links and has length in $3+I+I$, giving 5–7 in $H_7$ and 9–15 in $H_{15}$. A cycle through the center in $H'_7$ uses two subdivided spokes and one or two outer edges, so its length is 5 or 6.

Extending $t$ endpoints of a port pair shifts its path lengths up by $t$. Consequently **adding an edge between any two boundary vertices of an extended or unextended core creates a forbidden cycle**. For $T$, take an old path of length $3-t$, where $t=1$ or 2. For either seven-vertex core use length 3 when $t=0$, and $7-t$ otherwise. For $H_{15}$ use $7-t$ in every case.

**How the claim proves the theorem.** In the empty piece, adding root edges gives each boundary vertex degree at most two. In the star, two root edges already create a 4-cycle. In the four cores, even one root edge creates a forbidden cycle. Thus **every boundary vertex in a clean completion has degree at most two**. At the root triangle containing $x,y$, the third vertex has degree at least three in $H$. This proves the theorem.

## When two pieces meet

Two pieces on different faces of $abcd$ share a pair of boundary vertices. A path between those vertices in each piece gives a cycle with length the sum of the path lengths. If the pieces share all three boundary vertices, the same test works whenever one path avoids the third vertex.

For the star and triangle call a pair **short** when its path lengths are $\{2\}$ (star) or $[2,3]$ (one triangle port extended), and **long** when they are $[3,4]$ (both triangle ports extended). On different faces we have just two possibilities for nonempty pieces to coexist.

* A star or triangle can meet another star or triangle only with one short and one long pair. Two short pairs give length 4 and two long pairs give 8.
* The only other possibility is two copies of $H_7$ meeting on their outer pairs, with exactly three of the four incidences at the shared ports extended.

For the second assertion, an $H_{15}$ gives length 16 with any nonempty piece. The smallest possible sum of lengths is at most $7+7$, and the largest is at least $14+2$. A seven-vertex core gives length 8 with a star or triangle by the same interval test. Between two seven-vertex cores the sum interval is $[m_1+m_2+t,12+t]$, where $m_i$ is 2 or 3 before extension and $t$ counts the four possible extensions at the shared pair. Avoiding 8 and 16 forces $t=3$ and $m_1=m_2=3$.

**Combining pieces with the same boundary.** Discard empty inputs. Whenever the omitted third vertex is a leaf in either piece, the two-face test above applies to the other pair. This excludes $H_{15}$ or either seven-vertex core as soon as there is a leaf somewhere. Indeed, the sole exception in the test requires three extensions along the two shared outer pairs. We may then omit one of those leaves and test a different pair, which cannot also be the outer pair. With no leaves, both pieces are seven- or fifteen-vertex cores. Two sevens give $4+4$, two fifteens give $8+8$, and a seven and a fifteen give $4+12$, using the avoiding path in the first piece in each sum.

Two stars give length 4. A star can meet a triangle only if every triangle port is extended. Their union is $H'_7$. For two triangles, a common unextended port gives a pair short on both sides, with the third vertex a leaf. Otherwise the omitted vertex is a leaf on at least one side for each of the three pairs. Exactly one side of each pair must then be short. This would give three short pairs in total, while each triangle contributes zero or two.

**Introducing $d$.** Count the nonempty pieces among the three faces.

* **Zero.** All three edges from $d$ are needed, giving the star.
* **One**, say on $abd$. A core allows only the new edge $dc$. To give $d$ degree three, its old port must be unextended, so this simply extends that port. For a star, $dc$ and exactly one of $da,db$ are needed. Adding both $da,db$ would give length 4. The result is a triangle with two ports extended.
* **Two**, say on $abd,acd$. If both are $H_7$, they share their outer pairs, and no extra edge is allowed. Three shared incidences are extended. Since $d$ must have degree three, exactly one incidence there is extended and both incidences at $a$ are extended. Thus the two old outer ports on the $d$ side are joined by an edge, and the other two by a two-edge path through $a$. This is $H_{15}$, possibly extended at $b,c$.

  In the other case one face has a short pair and the other is a triangle with a long pair. If the short face is a triangle, no extra edge is possible, so its $d$ port must be unextended and its $a$ port extended. If the short face is a star, $d$ has degree two and the sole permitted edge joins $d$ to the star's private port. This makes a triangle containing $d$. Either way there are now two triangles, joined by an edge on the $d$ side and a two-edge path through $a$. This is $H_7$, possibly extended at its private ports.
* **Three.** Each face meets the other two on different pairs, so the exception for two $H_7$ outer pairs is impossible. All faces are stars or triangles. In each face choose a path between its two outside vertices avoiding $d$. The three paths form a cycle. Their possible lengths and the short pairs incident with $d$ are as follows.

| Face | Number of short pairs incident with $d$ | Outside path lengths avoiding $d$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Star | 2 | 2 |
| Triangle with $d$ unextended | 2 | 3 |
| Triangle with an outside port unextended | 1 | 2–3 |
| Triangle with all ports extended | 0 | 3–4 |

Every overlap has exactly one short side, so the three counts sum to three. With counts $1,1,1$ the path lengths can sum to $2+3+3=8$. With counts $2,1,0$ the first path has length 2 or 3. Complete the sum to 8 with $2+4$ or $2+3$, respectively. This finishes the induction and proves the claim.

## Exact counts and a consequence for separators

The classification is exact. The constructions above produce the star, the triangle with two leaves, $H'_7$ from the star and the triangle with three leaves, and $H_7$ and $H_{15}$ from their two-face constructions with private ports unextended. The one-face construction extends any further port. With ordered boundary there are $1+1+4+24+8+24=62$ pieces. The factor 24 allows three choices for the middle port and eight sets of extensions. The pieces have at most 15 interior vertices, all cubic. Exactly $8+4+60=72$ of the $62\cdot8=496$ root completions are clean. Those with all three boundary degrees two give just $T,H_7,H'_7,H_{15}$ up to isomorphism. Equivalently these are all clean graphs of minimum degree two with only three possible degree-two vertices, provided making those three vertices a clique preserves treewidth at most three. [Three-port constructions](RecursiveGadgets.md)

**Inside an unrestricted counterexample.** Let $S$ be any three vertices, and let $C$ be a component of $G-S$. If making $S$ a clique in $G[C\cup S]$ gives treewidth at most three, root that completion at $S$ and omit the three possible root edges. The classification applies regardless of the treewidth of $G$. Thus $C$ has at most 15 vertices, all cubic in $G$, and the six shapes give every possible attachment to $S$.

## A sharp example on 34 vertices

Start from $K_4$ on $A,B,C,D$. Replace $A,B$ by copies of $H_{15}$ and $C$ by a triangle. Attach the three old edges at each replaced vertex to distinct ports, with the middle ports facing each other on $AB$. The resulting graph is cubic and has $15+15+3+1=34$ vertices.

A cycle using an edge between blocks projects to a simple cycle of $K_4$. Indeed, a cycle crosses any block boundary an even number of times and the block has only three outgoing edges. Count one outgoing edge with each visited block. If the cycle visits both large blocks, its length is at least $4+4+1=9$. If it visits only one, it passes through its two outer ports and also visits $C,D$, giving at least $6+2+1=9$. The cores themselves are clean. A 16-cycle follows the triangle $ABC$ using internal paths of lengths $6,6,1$ and three connecting edges.

To see the width bound, first replace $A,B,C$ by triangles on their ports. Deleting $D$ from this skeleton leaves a six-cycle with a triangle attached on each of three disjoint edges. This has treewidth two. Adding $D$ to every bag gives width at most three. Each port triangle occurs in a bag, so there we can attach the width-three decomposition of $H_{15}$ rooted at its ports, as supplied by the piece construction. Minimum degree three gives the matching lower bound.

## Independent certificates

Lean checks the degrees of every clean root in the finite grammar using three degrees and the lengths up to 15 of paths between each pair of boundary vertices, recorded separately according to whether they visit the third vertex. These data suffice because any segment inside a piece has two boundary endpoints. Two separated segments on a simple crossing cycle or on a simple path between parent boundary vertices would require four distinct child boundary vertices. A crossing cycle of length at most 16 uses at most 15 edges inside. Replacing its segment by a path of the same length and the same use of the third vertex preserves simplicity. The same replacement works for the recorded paths. The Lean theorem is `Erdos64.TreewidthProfiles.clean_root_degrees` and its finite calculations use `native_decide`. The graph decomposition is proved above in prose. [Lean certificate](Erdos64/TreewidthProfiles.lean)

The C++ checker independently enumerates all compositions and tests label-preserving isomorphism for repeated profiles. It also checks the adjacency lists, elimination order and a 16-cycle for the 34-vertex example. [C++ checker](scripts/treewidth_three.cpp)

## What happens at width four

There is a 105-vertex graph of treewidth exactly four and minimum degree three whose shortest dyadic cycle has length 32. The example starts from a partial 3-tree $H$ with 102 cubic vertices, one vertex of degree four, and two vertices $x,y$ of degree two. Their graph distance is 16. The verifier gives a 22-step construction for $H$, checks an elimination ordering of width three, and adds the edge $xy$. This yields 104 vertices of degree three and one of degree four. An elimination order of width four is obtained by moving $x$ to the end of the original order. Exhaustive enumeration excludes cycles of lengths 4, 8 and 16, and a displayed list of 32 distinct vertices forms a cycle. The width-three theorem then forces treewidth exactly four. This example also explains why the two-exception theorem uses the treewidth of $H+xy$.

The 90-vertex example from the recursive substitution theorem has treewidth exactly five. Each of its six copies of $H_{15}$ contains a cycle through all three ports. Deleting other internal vertices and contracting the three segments of that cycle gives a triangle with those three ports. The resulting 18-vertex minor is the triangular expansion of $K_{3,3}$. The verifier checks all possible sets of eliminated vertices in this minor. After eliminating a set $S$, two remaining vertices are adjacent in the filled graph exactly when the original graph has a path between them with all internal vertices in $S$. Starting with the empty set, the calculation admits a new vertex when its remaining degree is at most four. It never reaches the whole vertex set. This proves the lower bound of five. An explicit successful elimination on the 90-vertex graph gives the matching upper bound.
