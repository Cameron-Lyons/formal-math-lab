# Quartic Pell substitutions through $(1,0,1)$ fixed by reflection

Let $u^2 - Dv^2 = 1$ for any nonsquare rational $D$, and consider a **reflection-symmetric quartic substitution** passing through the integer seed $(1, 0, 1)$ at $(u, v) = (1, 0)$:

$$
x = C(u), \qquad y = v E(u), \qquad z = J(u),
$$

where $C, E, J \in \mathbb{Q}[u]$ satisfy $\deg C = 4$, $\deg E \le 2$, $\deg J \le 6$, and $C(1) = J(1) = 1$. (Here "reflection-symmetric" means the conic involution $(u, v) \mapsto (u, -v)$ fixes $x$ and $z$ while negating $y$.) Putting $k = 1/D$ and using $v^2 = k(u^2 - 1)$, the cubic surface equation $x^3 + y^2 z + z^2 = 2$ becomes

$$
C^3 + J^2 + k(u^2 - 1) E^2 J = 2, \qquad k = \frac{1}{D}.
$$

What happens at the **second endpoint** $(u, v) = (-1, 0)$ of the Pell conic? Because $v = 0$, we automatically get $y = 0$, so $(-C(-1), J(-1))$ must be a rational point on the Mordell elliptic curve

$$
Y^2 = X^3 + 2.
$$

Its Mordell–Weil group is infinite cyclic generated by $P = (-1, 1)$, and its only integral points are $\pm P = (-1, \pm 1)$ (see [LMFDB curve 1728a1](https://www.lmfdb.org/EllipticCurve/Q/1728a1/)). Writing $(-C(-1), J(-1)) = nP$ for an integer $n$, this document proves:

1. **No integral second endpoint ($n \ne \pm 1$):** No such substitution can take an integer value at $u = -1$ — and in particular, **no substitution of this form can have integer coefficients**.
2. **Large rational-endpoint bound ($|n| > 100$):** Even allowing arbitrary rational values at $u = -1$, every candidate index with $|n| \le 100$ is ruled out!

## Proof roadmap

We break the proof into three self-contained parts:

1. **Multi-prime sieve for $1 < |n| \le 100$:** Balancing valuations at odd primes $p$ splits any rational substitution into three regimes ($h > 0$, $h < 0$, $h = 0$), restricting $n$ modulo the order of $P \bmod p$ and forcing $4 \mid v_p(C(-1))$ at $p = 13, 19$ whenever $C(-1)$ is nonintegral. Together with $p = 11, 47, 59, 61, 97$, this eliminates every $1 < |n| \le 100$.
2. **Equal integral endpoint ($n = 1$: $C(-1) = J(-1) = 1$):** At $p = 13$, Hensel rigidity forces the extra symmetry $u \mapsto -u$ ($C, J$ even and $E$ odd), collapsing $s = u^2$ into a pole-order-$2$ Pell family that contradicts [pole_two.md](pole_two.md).
3. **Opposite integral endpoint ($n = -1$: $C(-1) = 1, J(-1) = -1$):** At $p = 13$, reversing the polynomials in $s = 1/u$, shifting to a Hensel-lifted critical point $\alpha$ of $A'$, and zooming by $s = \alpha + \rho^2 T$ reduces the equation to a degree-$6$ identity over $\mathbb{F}_{13}[T]$ that Lean ([QuarticReflection.lean](QuarticReflection.lean)) proves has only the zero solution.

## Part 1: Reducing coefficients of arbitrary height ($|n| > 100$)

Shift the seed $u = 1$ to the origin by setting $t = u - 1$ and $H = k t(t + 2) E^2$. Then $C(0) = J(0) = 1$ and

$$
J(J + H) = 2 - C^3.
$$

For an odd prime $p$, let $h = \max_{i>0}(-v_p(a_i)/i)$ over all nonzero positive-degree coefficients of $C, J, H$. Pick $\lambda$ with $v_p(\lambda) = h$ in a totally ramified extension (a root of a uniformizer achieves any rational valuation while keeping the residue field $\mathbb{F}_p$). Then $C(\lambda T), J(\lambda T), H(\lambda T)$ have integral coefficients and at least one nonconstant term survives reduction.

* **$\bar H$ cannot vanish identically:** Otherwise $\bar C^3 + \bar J^2 = 2$ defines a smooth genus-$1$ curve over $\mathbb{F}_p$, which has only constant rational-function points by the constant-fiber theorem in [PellConstantFiber.lean](PellConstantFiber.lean).
* **Three scaling regimes:** Because the Gauss valuation of a square is twice that of its base, normalizing the reduction of $E(\lambda T)$ to be monic writes $\bar H = k' R e^2$ for some $k' \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times$ and monic $e \in \mathbb{F}_p[T]$ with $\deg e \le 2$, where the squarefree factor $R$ records the sign of $h$:
  * **$h > 0$ (positive scale):** $t(t + 2) = \lambda T(\lambda T + 2)$ is dominated by $2\lambda T$, so $R = T$.
  * **$h < 0$ (negative scale):** $\lambda T(\lambda T + 2)$ is dominated by $\lambda^2 T^2$, so $R = T^2$.
  * **$h = 0$ (unit scale, $\lambda = 1$):** $R = T(T + 2)$.

### Testing primes $11$, $13$, and $19$

For each of the $p^4$ candidate polynomials $C \in \mathbb{F}_p[T]$ with $C(0) = 1$, [quartic_reflection.py](quartic_reflection.py) factors $2 - C^3$, tests every divisor $J$ where both $J$ and $(2 - C^3)/J$ have degree at most $6$, scales $J$ so $J(0) = 1$, and checks whether $(2 - C^3)/J - J$ has the required form $k' R e^2$. The table counts nonconstant solutions with monic $e$ and translates them into allowed congruences for $n$:

| Prime | $R = T$ | $R = T^2$ | $R = T(T + 2)$ | Order of $P$ | Allowed indices $n$ modulo that order |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 11 | 0 | 140 | 46 | 12 | 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 |
| 13 | 12 | 0 | 30 | 19 | 0, 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 18 |
| 19 | 72 | 72 | 16 | 13 | 0, 1, 12 |

How do the three regimes constrain $nP \bmod p$? Since $11, 13, 19$ are primes of good reduction for $Y^2 = X^3 + 2$, $nP$ reduces to $n\bar P$:

* **Positive scale ($h > 0$):** At both $13$ and $19$, every positive-scale residue keeps full $\deg \bar C = 4$. At the second endpoint $t = -2$, the quartic term strictly dominates all lower terms, so the endpoint reduces to the point at infinity ($n \equiv 0$ modulo the order of $\bar P$).
* **Negative scale ($h < 0$):** Every nonconstant coefficient has positive valuation, so the second endpoint collapses onto the seed $P$ ($n \equiv 1$ modulo the order of $\bar P$).
* **Unit scale ($h = 0$):** Evaluating each surviving unit-scale model at $T = -2$ directly gives the remaining allowed residue classes of $n$.

### Two extra arithmetic constraints at $13$ and $19$

1. **Nonintegral $X$-valuations must be multiples of $4$:** At both $13$ and $19$, the surviving positive indices of $C, J, H$ in every positive-scale residue have $\gcd = 1$. Since any surviving index $i$ satisfies $v_p(a_i) = -ih \in \mathbb{Z}$, **$h$ must be an integer**. Consequently, every nonintegral endpoint at $13$ or $19$ has $v_p(C(-1)) = -4h$, a multiple of $4$!
2. **Formal-group reciprocity ($13 \mid n \iff 19 \mid n$):** Exact group addition on $Y^2 = X^3 + 2$ gives $v_{13}(X(19P)) = -2$ and $v_{19}(X(13P)) = -2$. At any prime of good reduction, in the formal parameter $-X/Y$ at infinity, multiplication by $m$ has leading term $mT$ with integral higher terms, and $v(X) = -2v(T)$. Thus if $13 \mid n$ and $19 \nmid n$, then $v_{19}(X(nP)) = v_{19}(X((n/13)(13P))) = -2$, contradicting divisibility by $4$! Swapping $13$ and $19$ proves the converse: any candidate must satisfy $13 \mid n \iff 19 \mid n$.

### Eliminating the last seven indices below 100

Checking the rational multiples $nP$ directly for $1 < |n| \le 100$, the mod-$11, 13, 19$ constraints together with the $4$-divisible valuation test leave only **seven** candidate indices: $-77, -53, -51, -27, 25, 27, 77$. Four targeted prime checks eliminate them all:

| Prime | Excluded indices | Distinct endpoint values $(C(-1), J(-1))$ tested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 47 | -77, -51, 77 | (6, 31), (6, 16) |
| 59 | -27, 25 | (28, 36), (43, 26) |
| 61 | -53 | (30, 5) |
| 97 | 27 | (46, 65) |

At each of these four primes, [quartic_reflection.py](quartic_reflection.py) verifies that:

* no positive-scale model loses both maximal degrees, so $h > 0$ would force a nonintegral endpoint (ruling out $h > 0$);
* each listed target point is finite and distinct from $(1, 1)$ (ruling out $h < 0$);
* for $h = 0$, prescribing $C(-1)$ adds a linear constraint on $C$, leaving only $p^3$ choices, and none admits a valid divisor $J$ hitting the target endpoint.

## Part 2: Equal integral endpoints ($C(-1) = J(-1) = 1$)

Suppose $C(-1) = J(-1) = 1$ ($n = 1$). At $p = 13$:

* the integral endpoint rules out $h > 0$,
* there are zero $h < 0$ residues (see table above), and
* only **two** unit-scale ($h = 0$) residues match $(1, 1)$ at $u = -1$.

In the original variable $u$, both surviving residues have **even** $C$ and $J$ and **odd** $e$. Fixing both endpoints gives $14$ equations ($12$ polynomial coefficient equations plus $2$ endpoint equations) in $13$ unknowns ($4$ nonconstant coefficients of $C(t)$, $6$ of $J(t)$, and $3$ of $e(t)$), and the $14 \times 13$ Jacobian has **full column rank $13$** modulo $13$.

In any $13$-adic lift, normalize $E$ to have a $13$-adic unit coefficient and absorb a unit square root via Hensel's lemma, fixing $k'$ to a chosen square-class representative in $\mathbb{Z}_{13}^\times$. Now apply the involution

$$
(C(u), J(u), e(u)) \mapsto (C(-u), J(-u), -e(-u)).
$$

It preserves the mod-$13$ residue, both exact endpoints, and the defining equation. Because the reduced Jacobian is injective, comparing base-$13$ digits inductively shows that any two lifts in the same class coincide — forcing $C$ and $J$ to be **even** and $E = e_1 u$ to be **odd** over $\mathbb{Q}$!

Finally, substitute $s = u^2$. This drops the degrees in half: $x$ and $z$ become polynomials in $s$ of degrees at most $2$ and $3$, while

$$
y^2 = \frac{e_1^2}{D} s(s - 1),
$$

with $e_1 \ne 0$ by the constant-fiber theorem. This defines a pointed conic with two nonsplit conjugate ends on which $x$ has pole order $2$ and $(s, y) = (1, 0)$ gives the integral seed — contradicting the pole-order-$2$ theorem in [pole_two.md](pole_two.md)!

## Part 3: Opposite integral endpoints ($C(-1) = 1, J(-1) = -1$)

Suppose $C(-1) = 1$ and $J(-1) = -1$ ($n = -1$). Again only $h = 0$ is possible at $p = 13$, leaving **two** residues, both with

$$
(\deg \bar C, \deg \bar J, \deg \bar E) = (2, 3, 0), \qquad \bar E \ne 0.
$$

Normalize $k$ to a unit in $\mathbb{Q}_{13}$ as before and reverse the polynomials in $s = 1/u$:

$$
A(s) = s^4 C(1/s), \qquad B(s) = s^6 J(1/s), \qquad F(s) = s^2 E(1/s).
$$

Multiplying the surface equation by $s^{12}$ gives

$$
A^3 + B^2 + k(1 - s^2) F^2 B = 2 s^{12}.
$$

Because $(\deg \bar C, \deg \bar J, \deg \bar E) = (2, 3, 0)$, the vanishing orders of $(A, B, F)$ at $s = 0$ modulo $13$ are $(2, 3, 2)$.

### Shifting to a Hensel-lifted critical point and zooming

1. **Kill the linear term of $A$:** Since $\bar A$ vanishes to exact order $2$ at $s = 0$, $\bar A'$ has a **simple** root at $0$ modulo $13$. By Hensel's lemma, this lifts to an exact root $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{13}$ of $A'$ with $v(\alpha) > 0$. Expand $A, B, F$ in powers of $s - \alpha$ with coefficients $A_i, B_i, F_i$ (so $A_1 = 0$ identically!).
2. **Choose the weighted zoom scale:** Define (with $v(0) = +\infty$)

   $$
   r = \min\left\{\frac{v(A_0)}{2},\, \frac{v(B_0)}{3},\, \frac{v(B_1)}{2},\, v(B_2),\, \frac{2v(F_0)}{3},\, 2v(F_1),\, 2v(\alpha)\right\}.
   $$

   Every entry is strictly positive (since all those coefficients vanish modulo $13$), and $r$ is finite (otherwise $\alpha = 0$ and $A_0 = A_1 = 0$, giving $s^2 \mid A$ and contradicting $\deg C = 4$).
3. **Zoom by $s = \alpha + \rho^2 T$:** Pick $\rho$ with $v(\rho) = r/2$ in a totally ramified extension and substitute $s = \alpha + \rho^2 T$. Then $A/\rho^4$, $B/\rho^6$, and $F/\rho^3$ are all integral and reduce modulo the maximal ideal to polynomials $a, b, f \in \mathbb{F}_{13}[T]$ of degrees $2$, $3$, and at most $1$, with **no linear term in $a$** (since $A_1 = 0$). Dividing the reversed equation by $\rho^{12}$, all higher terms vanish and the right-hand side $2(\alpha + \rho^2 T)^{12}/\rho^{12}$ reduces to the constant $2c^6$, where $c = \overline{(\alpha/\rho)^2}$. By the choice of $r$, **at least one** of $a(0)$, the lower coefficients of $b$ (below degree $3$), the coefficients of $f$, or $c$ is nonzero!
4. **Complete the square and apply Lean's finite-field contradiction:** Put $w = 2b + \bar k f^2$, turning the reduced equation into

   $$
   w^2 = 8c^6 + \bar k^2 f^4 - 4a^3.
   $$

   Matching leading ($T^6$) terms gives $w_3^2 = -4a_2^3$, so scaling $T$ normalizes the leading coefficients of $w$ and $a$ to $2$ and $-1$. Matching the $T^5$ coefficient immediately forces $w_2 = 0$ (since $a$ has no linear term and $\deg f^4 \le 4$), leaving the explicit identity over $\mathbb{F}_{13}[T]$:

   $$
   (2T^3 + dT + e)^2 = 8c^6 + \bar k^2 (mT + g)^4 - 4(-T^2 + a_0)^3.
   $$

Lean proves in [QuarticReflection.lean](QuarticReflection.lean) that this finite-field identity forces

$$
a_0 = d = e = m = g = c = 0,
$$

which makes $a$ and $b$ pure monomials and kills every single term that could have attained the minimum $r$! This contradiction rules out the opposite endpoint $n = -1$.

* **Verification split:** The Hensel shift, rescaling, and specialization are written steps above; Lean checks the leading-coefficient scaling and the finite-field contradiction in [QuarticReflection.lean](QuarticReflection.lean), and FLINT checks all finite-field searches in [quartic_reflection.py](quartic_reflection.py).
