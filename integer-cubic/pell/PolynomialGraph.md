# Polynomial graphs and linear Pell ordinates

Suppose $f\in\mathbb Q[Y]$ and $Y^4+8-4f(Y)^3=q(Y)e(Y)^2$ for $q,e\in\mathbb Q[Y]$ with $\deg q\le2$. If both $y_0$ and $f(y_0)$ are integral at 7 for some $y_0\in\mathbb Q$, then **$\deg f\le2$**. In particular, a polynomial graph of degree three or greater through an integer solution cannot produce a Pell equation by extracting a polynomial square. The degree bound in characteristic zero and the two contradictions after rescaling below are verified in Lean. The finite search and the passage to the residue field are separate parts of the proof. [Lean proofs](PolynomialGraph.lean) · [Exact finite search](polynomial_graph_mod7.py)

There is also a consequence for unrestricted polynomial substitutions on $u^2-Dv^2=1$, with $D$ nonsquare in $\mathbb Q$. If such a substitution is nonconstant and takes an integer value in all three coordinates at a rational parameter, **$y$ cannot have pole order one at the two ends**, irrespective of the degree of $x$. The last section explains how the geometry of the surface removes the polynomial-graph assumption in this case.

## A finite degree bound

Put $H=Y^4+8$ and $n=\deg f$. Assume $n\ge2$, so $\deg(qe^2)=3n$. Differentiating $H=4f^3+qe^2$ gives
$$3Hf'-H'f=e(3qe f'-q'ef-2qfe').$$
The left side has degree $n+3$ and leading coefficient $(3n-4)\operatorname{lc}(f)$. Thus $\deg e\le n+3$ and $3n\le2+2(n+3)$ gives $n\le8$. [Degree-bound theorem](PolynomialGraph.lean)

## Coefficient integrality and the six reductions

Use the absolute value on $\overline{\mathbb Q}_7$ with $|7|<1$. Translate to the given $y_0$ and expand $f(y_0+s)=\sum a_i s^i$. If any $|a_i|>1$, choose $0<|\lambda|<1$ so $\max_{i>0}|a_i\lambda^i|=1$. The constant term is integral and the reduced polynomial $A(t)$ of $f(y_0+\lambda t)$ is nonconstant. All required radii are rational powers of $|7|$ and are available in finite extensions. The reduction of $H(y_0+\lambda t)$ is a nonzero constant $h$, since $Y^4+8$ has no root over $\mathbb F_7$. The polynomial $h-4A^3$ is a square times a polynomial of degree at most two. Indeed the Gauss norm is multiplicative: normalize the two factors of $qe^2$ to have norm one before reducing, absorbing a unit into one factor. Over an algebraic closure, a curve $v^2=q_0(t)$ with $\deg q_0\le2$ is rational on each component that dominates the $t$-line. Pullback to that component would give a nonconstant rational point on $w^2+4a^3=h$ over a rational function field. The characteristic-seven constant-fiber theorem excludes it. Therefore the translated coefficients are integral; translating back by the integral $y_0$ gives integral coefficients of $f$. [Constant-fiber theorem](PellConstantFiber.lean)

Now reduce $f$ in its original variable. For any $\bar f$ of degree at most eight, the search factors $Y^4+8-4\bar f^3$ up to removal of all even factor multiplicities. It first tests divisibility by a sufficiently large square using the gcd with the derivative. This necessary test also holds for inseparable polynomials. Normalizing the leading coefficient under multiplication of $\bar f$ by a cube root of unity gives representatives 1 and 3. At even degree, a sign change of $Y$ also normalizes the first nonzero odd coefficient. The zero polynomial is checked separately. The complete list left by these operations is
$$Y+5,\quad 3Y+6,\quad Y^2+2Y+1,\quad Y^2+2,\quad Y^2+Y+4,\quad 3Y^2+1.$$
Every member leaves a squarefree factor of degree exactly two. Consequently, in any rational lift $q$ also has degree two and both roots are bounded at 7. To see this, normalize $q,e$ by their Gauss norms. The reduced $q$ must account for both distinct roots, so its degree remains two with unit leading coefficient. [Enumeration](polynomial_graph_mod7.py)

## The degree cannot increase in a lift

Suppose first that $\bar f$ is quadratic but $n>2$. Then $f$ has a root $\gamma$ with $R=|\gamma|>1$. Otherwise the nonunit leading coefficient and the factorization into bounded roots would make all coefficients nonunits. The Gauss norm of $f(\gamma+\gamma T)$ equals that of $f(\gamma T)$, by integral translation of $T$ and its inverse, and is at least $R^2$ by the unit quadratic coefficient. At radius zero the value is $f(\gamma)=0$. By the maximum formula for the Gauss norm of a polynomial on a disc, there is a radius $0<|\lambda|<R$ at which the norm of $f(\gamma+\lambda T)$ is $R^{4/3}$. Divide by a cube root of $\gamma^4$ and reduce to obtain a nonconstant polynomial $A$ with zero constant coefficient.

On this disc $H(\gamma+\lambda T)/\gamma^4$ reduces to 1. Both roots of $q$ are bounded, so $q(\gamma+\lambda T)/q(\gamma)$ also reduces to 1. Choose a square root of $q(\gamma)$ and divide the original identity by $\gamma^4$. The rescaled $e$ is integral by multiplicativity of the Gauss norm, and its reduction $B$ satisfies $B^2+4A^3=1$. This again forces $A$ to be constant.

If $\bar f$ is linear and $n\ge2$, use discs centered at zero. For $R>1$ sufficiently near one, $\|f\|_R/R^{4/3}<1$, since just the constant and linear coefficients can be units. For large $R$ the ratio exceeds 1. Choose $R=|\lambda|>1$ at equality, scale $Y=\lambda T$ and divide the identity by $\lambda^4$. The normalized $f$ has nonzero reduction $A$. Both branch roots of $q$ are bounded, so its normalized reduction is a nonzero multiple of $T^2$. Over the algebraic closure we obtain $B^2+4A^3=T^4$. Substitute $T=s^3$ and divide by $s^{12}$. The constant-fiber theorem forces $A(s^3)/s^4$ to be constant. Its numerator is a nonzero polynomial in $s^3$, which cannot be a nonzero scalar times $s^4$. These two contradictions prove the polynomial-graph claim. [Formal residue identities](PolynomialGraph.lean)

## Why a linear Pell ordinate forces a polynomial graph

The generic plane fiber, at $y=Y$, is the elliptic curve $E/\mathbb Q(Y)$ given by $(2z+Y^2)^2=Y^4+8-4x^3$. Its only rational point over this function field is its point at infinity $O$. Here are the details of the geometric argument.

The smooth projective cubic surface $S$ is $x^3+y^2z+z^2w=2w^3$. Its 27 geometric lines form two Galois orbits of sizes 3 and 24. The former are the plane section $z=0$; the latter have slopes with irreducible polynomial $T^{24}+540T^{12}-108$. The geometric Picard group is torsion-free and generated by the classes of the 27 lines. Their sum equals $9H$ for $H$ the hyperplane class, as is also seen by summing the 45 tritangent plane sections, each line occurring five times. Averaging over the finite permutation group shows that the invariant Picard space over $\mathbb Q$ is spanned by the two orbit sums $H,8H$. Since $H$ intersects a line in degree one, every invariant integral class is an integral multiple of $H$. [Line equations and factorization](QuarticGeometry.md) · [Huybrechts, *The Geometry of Cubic Hypersurfaces*, section 3.4](https://www.math.uni-bonn.de/people/huybrech/Notes.pdf)

A section distinct from $O$ would give a rational curve $C$ on $S$ with class $rH$. On the smooth generic plane cubic its intersection divisor is $P+(3r-1)O$: off $w=0$ the section meets the generic fiber at its defining point with multiplicity one, and $O=[0:0:1:0]$ is its only point at infinity. This divisor is equivalent to $3rO$, since the line at infinity cuts out $3O$. Thus $P-O$ is principal, forcing $P=O$. The argument can be made after extending the constant field to $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, where the stated divisor class equality holds.

On a nonsplit Pell conic with $y$ of pole order one at each end, $y$ has degree two as a map to the projective line. Its function field is a quadratic extension of $\mathbb Q(y)$ with nontrivial automorphism $\sigma$. The point $P=(x,2z+y^2)$ of the generic elliptic fiber satisfies $P+\sigma P=O$ by the preceding paragraph. Elliptic inversion changes the sign of $2z+y^2$ and fixes $x$. Therefore $x\in\mathbb Q(y)$, and $x$ is a polynomial $f(y)$ because all of its poles lie over infinity. The anti-invariant coordinate $2z+y^2$ has square $H-4f^3$. The quadratic extension is the function field of a genus-zero curve, so its squarefree radicand has at most two geometric branch points. Equivalently $H-4f^3=q e^2$ with $\deg q\le2$; if the coordinate were zero, $H=4f^3$ would contradict squarefreeness of $H$. The marked integral point supplies the required $y_0$. The graph bound implies that $x$ has pole order at most two, and the earlier pole-order theorem forces the map to be constant. [Pole-order-two argument](pole_two.md)

## A bound when both coordinates have larger pole order

For any nonconstant Pell substitution write $n$ and $m$ for the pole orders of $x$ and $y$ at either conjugate end. Then **$n\le8m$**. Here $m>0$, since a constant $y$ gives a constant map on its smooth elliptic fiber. The assertion is immediate when $3n\le4m$. Otherwise put $w=2z+y^2$. The identity $w^2+4x^3=y^4+8$ forces $w$ to have pole order $3n/2$ at both ends. Over the quadratic splitting field choose a Laurent parameter $t$ with ends $0,\infty$. Write $a=t^nx$, $b=t^my$, $c=t^{3n/2}w$. These ordinary polynomials have degrees $2n,2m,3n$ and nonzero constant coefficients. With $k=3n-4m>0$ and $h=b^4+8t^{4m}$ they satisfy
$$c^2+4a^3=t^k h.$$
Set $\delta=t\,d/dt$. Differentiating shows that $c$ divides $N=3h\delta a-a(kh+\delta h)$, since it divides $t^k N$ and is coprime to $t$. The constant coefficient $-k a(0)h(0)$ is nonzero, so $3n=\deg c\le\deg N\le2n+8m$. This restricts an abscissa accompanying an ordinate of order two to order at most 16.
